# Yorimichi · 寄り道

An original prototype of a playable top-down travel journal set in Japan. Phaser 3, TypeScript and Vite. Scenery and characters are drawn in code, with no external assets. Maps are 480 × 320 pixels, scaled up without smoothing; the traveller is animated in four directions.

## Development

Node.js 22.12+ (tested with Node 24).

```sh
npm ci --cache /workspace/.npm-cache
npm run dev
```

`npm run build` checks TypeScript and then produces `dist/`. `npm run preview` serves the build. No application server, account or secret is needed.

## Controls

Tap/click the map to walk around buildings; tap/click an element (ticket machine, staff, gates, exits, return sign, camera) to walk to it and use it. On the keyboard, the arrows or **WASD** (keys read by physical position: ZQSD on AZERTY) move the character, and **E**, **Space** or **Enter** interact. On a gamepad, the left stick or D-pad walks, **A** interacts, **X** opens the inventory, **Y** the journal, **RB** the phone and **Start** the menu (**Esc** on the keyboard, or the Menu button in the top band), where you can restart from zero; in dialogs and on the departures board, the D-pad chooses, **A** confirms and **B** closes. The **right mouse button** does whatever **B** does, everywhere (back, cancel, leave a view). Everything you can use is outlined, fading between white and yellow, here and in the ticket hall; whatever is within reach turns yellow. On a phone, landscape is recommended. **Inventory** opens your bag: the tickets you carry (you can hold several, each drawn as a JR ticket; they are kept on the device, `localStorage['yorimichi-save']`, until you board with them, along with where you stand: a reload puts you back on the same spot) and your wallet; **Journal** shows the three photo memories you can collect on excursions.

## Home in Higashi-Nakano

The traveller lives in **Higashi-Nakano** (東中野), on the yellow Chuo-Sobu local between Nakano and Shinjuku, and a rental waits for them there: a new journey starts at 9:00 at **Tokyo Station**, just in from Haneda Airport, with a popup that sends you to Higashi-Nakano and tells you to collect the journal's memories. Higashi-Nakano is a station like the others (no Shinkansen: the NewDays on the right). Out of its **west exit**, the street in front of the station has a blue 東中野一丁目 address sign at the left edge of the plaza: it leads to the **home street**, a quiet residential lane a screen and a half long that scrolls sideways as you walk: houses behind their block walls, utility poles with their street lights, a coin park, vending machines, a side street with its painted とまれ, lit windows at night. At its far end stands the apartment block, コーポ ヨリミチ; door **102** is yours. Inside, a 1LDK in the same three-quarter view as the street, scrolling up and down: from the genkan by the front door, the entrance corridor with the toilet on one side and the washroom (and the bath beyond it) on the other; at its end the living room, its window down to the floor onto the balcony, the TV, the sofa and the low table, and its open kitchen behind an island counter; through a sliding door, the bedroom with the bed, a desk and the closet. The windows darken as night falls, and you walk behind the sofa, the island and the fridge. Lie down on the **bed** to sleep: the screen fades out and you get up at 9:00 the next morning (the next 9:00, whatever the time). The lane starts on the right, where you come in from the station's street (its sign is at that street's left edge): the green 東中野駅 sign at that end walks back to it. Lorenzo's Uber, once he is in Japan, also drives you home from any station around Tokyo (see the phone). Kyoto has no home yet.

## The phone

Your phone sits in the bottom right corner of the screen, a red badge counting unread messages. Click it (or **P**, or **RB** on a gamepad) to take it out: an Android-like home screen with the time on the journey's clock, and **Messages**, the one app that works for now. Three conversations wait there: Jennifer, Namiko and Lorenzo. Lorenzo is in Hong Kong for work; his conversation offers a ready-made reply asking him to come to Japan. Send it and he answers **OK!**, then the phone is put away and his flight plays: a pixel map of East Asia, his plane taking off from Hong Kong and landing at Tokyo Haneda (or at Kansai when you are on the Kyoto side; a click, a key or **A** skips it). Back with you, he has taken an Uber to the station nearest you: in front of that station, you see the black car pull up just past the crossing, then his message arrives (*I'm at … Station!*); anywhere else the message comes at once and the Uber is already waiting when you walk out in front of that station, by either exit. The car is outlined like everything else you can use: get in and Lorenzo's Uber **drives you home**, to the apartment in Higashi-Nakano, free: Lorenzo pays (the clock runs on by the ride, about 2.4 minutes a km through town, and it runs at night too, when the stations are shut). You get out at your door in the home street, the Uber drives off, and Lorenzo, dropped at Higashi-Nakano Station, waits there until you call him again. From the Kyoto side Tokyo is too far by car: he tells you to take the Shinkansen. Once he is in Japan, you can call him again whenever you like: his conversation then offers *Can you come and pick me up?*; he answers *OK! On my way.* and a moment later his Uber is at the station nearest you, as above (no flight this time), or, if he already waits there, he says so. In the phone, the arrows or D-pad move between buttons, **A** / Enter presses, **B** / Backspace goes back a screen and **Esc**, **P** or **RB** puts it away. Conversations and Lorenzo's trip are kept with the save.

## Every station

Every station on a local line of the game (198 of them, around Tokyo and around Kyoto) is a full station like Tokyo: the concourse with its machine wall, ticket office and exits, the central gates, the corridor behind them with a staircase for each line, and the platforms. They all share one layout; what changes is their name and colour, their lines and track numbers, and the right of the concourse: the Shinkansen transfer gates where a Shinkansen stops (Tokyo, Ueno, Omiya, Shinagawa, Kyoto, Shin-Osaka), a NewDays everywhere else. Their exits lead to the street outside, except Kamakura's east exit (the temple trail) and Saga-Arashiyama's (the bamboo path). Track numbers are fictional: a pair per line, lines of one colour sharing an island platform.

Fares are worked out as JR does: from the shortest operating km between the two stations along the lines, by the March 2026 JR East bands. The km from Tokyo come from the station tables, and the result gives back exactly the 30 fares of the official Tokyo board; around Kyoto, the km are rounded from JR West's. So every station's machines sell the fares from there, and the Tokyo-area board (on the wall and on the machine) shows the fares from the station you stand in, marked in red. The Kyoto area has no board yet: ask the station staff for a fare. There is no local fare between the two areas.

## Tokyo JR map and tickets

Tokyo is a Chuo-Sobu ride (and a change) away from home. Interacting in front of the machine wall at the back of the hall opens a **front view of the ticket machines**; the player starts in front of a local ticket machine. Using the selected machine opens its screen. It follows the blue look of the ordinary machines documented by JR West, in Japanese by default with an **English** button.

1. Choose **Purchase Ticket → Cash · IC Card**.
2. Look up at the **large wall map**, which holds 31 JR stations and their fares from the station you are in. **Look at the wall map**, above the machine (or **Y** on a gamepad), pauses the purchase and opens the board above the machines full size. Drag to look around, move sideways to read another part, wheel or up/down to step closer/back. When you look up at it, the map gets a yellow frame: a click, **E** or **A** opens it full size ("read the stations").
3. Read your station's fare, then look down and click (or tap) the machine: it lights up on hover, opens if you stand in front of it, and otherwise you walk up to it. **E** or **A** do the same with the machine in front of you, framed in yellow. Coming back to the same machine keeps the selection and the purchase step. Every fare to a station the local lines reach can be bought, from ¥160 to ¥2,420 at Tokyo.
4. Choosing an amount goes straight to payment (**Back** returns to the amounts). Pay with the coins and notes of your **wallet** (see below). The screen shows the amount due, the amount inserted and what is still to pay.
5. Take the printed ticket from the きっぷ outlet (the screen only thanks you and shows the change), for example **TOKYO → ¥260**, and your change from the おつり outlet. The ticket has no preselected destination.
6. Go through the local gates on the left (see **Ticket gates** below), walk down the corridor to the stairs of your destination's line (see **To the platforms**), queue for its train the right way, and once aboard choose the station to get off at (see **On the platform**). A ticket that is too cheap still boards, as in Japan: the exit gates at your destination refuse it until you pay the rest at the **fare adjustment machine** beside them (see **Leaving the platforms**). Any overpayment is not refunded.

## Ticket gates

Using the 在来線 or 新幹線 gates on the map opens a still **pixel-art close-up of one gate**, seen from where you stand, after the JR East gates at Shinjuku's west gate: charcoal and green cabinets whose black top slopes down at the near end into a hood with a small screen, an IC reader and a yellow きっぷ slot on the cabinet on your right, the flaps between, the yellow 改札 band and the platform board on the wall behind. You cannot move or look around. Your **inventory** stands beside the view: your tickets in their own pocket, as many as you carry (each drawn as a JR magnetic ticket, in JR East green, JR West blue from Kyoto or JR Central orange on the Shinkansen) and the wallet. **Drag a ticket onto the slot** (or click it in the inventory; clicking the slot itself does nothing, the ticket has to be chosen). With several tickets, choose one with the D-pad, the left stick or the arrows (it is framed in yellow) and put it in with **A** or **E**. The gate reads it; if it is valid, a pixel fade takes you through to a second picture, over the exit slot (出口), where the ticket comes out, **punched** (a round hole, kept on it afterwards and shown in the inventory): **click it** (or **E** / **A**) to take it back, and you are in the corridor to the platforms; through the Shinkansen transfer gates there is no corridor: you come straight up onto your ticket's track (see **The Shinkansen**). A ticket for another platform or line, or one already punched, is refused: beep beep, the flaps shut, a red cross on the little screen, and the ticket comes back out of the slot. The gate does not say why: check the ticket and the signs. Nothing takes you to the ticket machines: walking back to them is up to you. **Esc** or **B** goes back to the concourse (a refused ticket is picked up first; at the exit slot it takes the ticket and goes on). On the way in, the punch records the station: the ticket knows where its trip began.

## To the platforms

Past the gates, a **corridor** seen from above runs up under the tracks, away from the gates. Every JR line stopping at the station (the same lines as on the fare map) has its island platform, reached by a **staircase on either side** of the corridor, the left one signed with the platform's first track, the right one with its second, each under a small sign in the **line's colour** with its track number and the line's name in Japanese and English; lines sharing a platform (Utsunomiya and Takasaki at Tokyo) share its stairs and its sign. Where the station has many lines, the corridor is taller than the screen and **scrolls** as you walk. Travellers walk it too: off their trains down the stairs and out through the gates, sometimes stopping at the fare adjustment machine or the staffed window first, in through the gates and up to a platform, or across to another platform, now and then pausing to read the signs; as on the concourse, they jostle rather than block, and nobody takes the machine or window you stand at. Go up the stairs of your line (**E**, **A** or a click) to its platform (see **On the platform**). Walking back to the gates (出口) opens the gate view again, the other way (see **Leaving the platforms**).

## On the platform

Up the stairs, the **island platform** seen from above, two screens long: it **scrolls sideways** as you walk, and you come up at its left or right end, as the corridor's stairs you took. The first track runs along the top, the second along the bottom, each with a white edge line and a yellow tactile strip; a stairwell goes down at each end, back to the corridor's stairs on that side. In the middle stands the station sign (駅名標: the station's name, and the stops either side on the line's colour with their English names), which people pass behind, and beside it the **route map** (路線図), a low board read from either side: from the top side, the line as the first track's trains run it, from the bottom side as the second's (here in red, the stops they go to in the line's colour, the others greyed; one row per line sharing the platform). The Yamanote Line, a loop, is drawn as its ring, roughly as it lies on the map, arrows round it the way the track's trains go. Near each stairwell, a small departure board hangs over each edge (you walk under it), its arrow pointing to its track: the track's number, the next train's time on the journey's clock (when it leaves: it pulls in in time and does not leave before), where it goes and its line, in Japanese and English by turns. As a train pulls out, its board turns to the next one, on the next line where several share the platform. After the last train (0:45) the boards say the service has ended, until the first one (4:40). Each track's trains run one way: the first towards the start of the line's list of stops in `src/rail.ts` (the Yamanote's outer loop, 外回り), the second towards its end (the inner loop); at a terminus both leave the only way there is. Lines sharing a platform take turns.

A train pulls in (announced in the top band), stops with its doors on the pale green **乗車位置** marks on the floor (a lane marked out either side of each door, footprints where each row stands, the middle kept clear) and opens them: people **get off first**, through the middle (stand there and they knock you aside, dazed for a moment, and it takes another click or **E** to board), and make for the stairs, then those **queuing** at each mark, in two lines inside its lanes as on Japanese platforms, step in, the two lines taking turns while those behind move up. **Click the standing train** to walk to the nearest door's queue: you board on your own when your turn comes, as soon as the doors are open if nobody is getting off there (a click on the platform only walks). With the keyboard or a gamepad, stand still in a queue: you board when your turn comes, those ahead of you stepping in quicker (**E** or **A** at the **乗車 / Board** prompt, the only prompt on the platform, boards too; a standing train's open doors are outlined). Once you have chosen where to get off (below), the doors shut at once and the train pulls out, leaving anyone still queuing for the next one; otherwise the doors shut once the queues are in and the train leaves; miss it and the next one comes straight after. People keep coming up the stairs to queue, and jostle rather than block, as everywhere.

As soon as you are on board, the doors held open, the **line map** over the doors opens: the stops still ahead, from 現在地 (in red) to the end of the run. Every station of the game can be chosen (on the Shinkansen, the stations it shares with a local line), the others are greyed; **Stay at …: get off** steps you back onto the platform by the door you came in (B on a gamepad), and the train leaves without you. On the Yamanote loop the map is its ring, with arrows the way the train goes, and each stop says how many stops away it is: going the wrong way round still gets you there, the long way. Choosing a stop shuts the doors and the train pulls out, then you are on that stop's platform as the train pulls in, and you step off with the others; standing still there does not put you back on that train, which leaves without you (the next one to pull in boards you as usual). Down the stairs: at Tokyo, Kyoto or Kamakura, the corridor behind the gates; at another station, the choice between the way out and its other lines (のりかえ: their platform, without leaving the gates); at Saga-Arashiyama, the bamboo path.

## Time, night and closing hours

The game keeps its own **clock**, shown at the top left of the game's top band (and in the headers of the ticket hall and the gates): a date and time that run 30 times faster than real time (a game minute every two seconds, a day in 48 minutes), and keep running while you stand at a machine or switch to another window or tab (but not while the page is closed). A new journey starts at 9:00, as does every morning after a night in the apartment's bed; the time is saved with the journey. Riding a train moves the clock on by the ride's length (about 90 km/h between stations and a minute and a half at each; the Shinkansen's own times). For testing, **T** (or a click on the clock) asks for a time and sets the clock to it, the trains on the platform retimed; `?time=23:40` in the URL does the same at load.

From 18:00 night falls out of doors (the street and the exits' maps), fully dark by 19:00, until dawn between 5:00 and 6:00: the scene turns deep blue but where the lamps shine (the konbini's window, the vending machines, the shops' signs and some of the station's windows, the lanterns on the temple trail); the platforms dim by half, and the concourses and corridors stay lit. The entrance hall beyond the exit gates shows the night too. The **konbini** and its ATM stay open around the clock. The **stations** close from 1:00 to 4:30: whoever is still inside then (off the train) is seen out by the west exit, a ticket punched there unpunched and the ticket of a finished trip kept; outside, the shutters are down over the entrance until the station opens again.

## Leaving the platforms

Off a train, you come down the stairs of your platform into the corridor behind the gates, ticket still punched, at the foot of that platform's stairs: the other lines' stairs are there too, to change trains without going out. The way out is the **same gate view, facing the other way**: beyond it, the station's **entrance hall**: a wide floor, pillars, yellow exit signs hanging over the way (西口 / 東口 at the big stations), and at the far end the glass doors onto the street in daylight, under the station's name. Put the ticket in: the gate checks the trip from the station that punched it to here. A ticket that covers it is **kept by the gate** (its trip is over) and you walk out, to the concourse in front of the gates. A fare ticket that is too cheap, or a ticket with no entry record, gets the red cross. Back at the station you came in by without travelling, the gate refuses the ticket too, as real ones do: see the staff.

Inside, beside the gates on the left, the station's ticket office (みどりの窓口, the same one you see from the concourse) has its counters on this side too: this **staffed window** is the way out when something went wrong. The agent cancels the entry of a ticket punched at this very station (入場取消: it comes back unpunched, good for another trip), keeps the ticket of a trip that ended here, and lets you out through the side gate, whatever happened (for now without charging a missing fare).

The **fare adjustment machine** (のりこし精算機, the yellow 精算 sign) stands against the right wall just before the gates. Drag your ticket from the wallet panel onto its ticket slot (or click it): it shows where you got on, the fare, what your ticket paid and what is left. Pay the difference with coins and notes and take the **精算券** (fare adjustment ticket, cream paper) from the ticket outlet, with your change: the gate takes it on your way out. A ticket that already covers the trip, or that it cannot adjust, comes straight back out with a note on the screen.

From Tokyo with a fare ticket, take a line that gets you there with the fewest changes, as the fare map shows: Kamakura is direct on the Yokosuka Line (the Tokaido Line does not stop there: get off at Ofuna and change), Kofu is the Chuo Line with a change at Takao, Motoyawata a change onto the yellow Chuo-Sobu local from any of the lines that meet it. Nothing stops you boarding the wrong train: get off where you can and ride back. The station staff's fare hint names the line.

The fare map draws the same lines: where several run side by side (the Keihin-Tohoku, Tokaido and Yokosuka lines along the Yamanote loop, the Chuo rapid and the yellow local to Mitaka, the Sobu rapid and local to Chiba), each keeps its own stroke in its colour, as on the boards above real JR machines; a station where several stop has one box across them and its name once.

Search by name was removed from the machine to make reading the map part of the game. The **駅員** station staff, at the みどりの窓口 ticket office on the left, beside the central gates, offer an optional **fare hint** on request. The hint neither buys nor fills in a ticket.

The map is **JR only**, with no subway or private railways. It reproduces the 30 stations of an official JR East diagram, not every station in Tokyo, plus Kamakura on the Yokosuka Line; the other stops of its lines are drawn small, with their fare. The reference fares are those proposed for 2026 in a document published in December 2024: whether they are currently in effect has not been checked. See [REFERENCES.md](REFERENCES.md) for the source and the adaptations.

## Wallet and physical payment

The traveller carries a purse of real yen pieces: ¥1, ¥5, ¥10, ¥50, ¥100 and ¥500 coins, ¥1,000, ¥5,000 and ¥10,000 notes. It is kept on the device (`localStorage['yorimichi-wallet']`) and shown at the top of the screen.

The ATM seen through the window of the konbini outside each station is the real way to get cash. It opens a terminal after the Seven Bank machines that take cards issued abroad (the plate reads the chain's bank: Seven Bank, E-net or Lawson Bank): choose **お引出し / Withdrawal**, drag your debit card from the wallet onto the card slot (or click it), type the PIN written on the memo in your wallet (on screen or with the number keys), pick an amount (1 to 10 万円, or **その他の金額** to type it with the 万 and 千円 keys, up to ¥100,000), confirm the ¥110 fee, say whether you want a receipt, then take your card, then the notes (¥10,000 and ¥1,000) and the receipt. **残高照会** shows the balance; deposits and transfers are refused with a foreign card and the card comes back. Your account starts at ¥300,000 and is kept on the device (`localStorage['yorimichi-bank']`); cash left in the outlet when you walk away is drawn back in and credited again. Japanese or English, keyboard and gamepad as at the ticket machines, **Esc** to leave.

Through the konbini's door beside the ATM (or the NewDays in a station without a Shinkansen), you stand inside in first person, in pixel art like the ticket gates, before one aisle at a time: the bread shelf's sweet buns and pastries (あまいパン: melon pan, cream buns, croissants, doughnuts), its sandwiches and savoury breads (サンド・パン: ham and cheese, egg, curry bread, yakisoba bread), the onigiri (おにぎり: rice balls, hand rolls, sushi boxes), the crisps (ポテトチップス), the drinks fridge (ドリンク) and the alcohol fridge (お酒: beer, chūhai, sake, umeshu, shochu). Point at a product, or move along the shelves with the arrows (A D W S, the D-pad, the stick), to read its name and price (tax included: a base price mostly ending in 8 plus 8% on food and drink, 10% on alcohol, so ¥149 rather than ¥150, and yes, you will collect ¥1 coins). Each chain prices things a little differently, the same in all its shops, so it can pay to compare. Going left or right past the end of a shelf walks to the next aisle; **LB / RB** or the buttons at the foot of the picture go there at once. A click (**E**, **Enter** or **A**) puts it in the basket; the cart at the top right (**C** or **Y**) shows everything in the basket, the chosen product up close with its name on the package, to be put back or kept, and a button that goes straight to the till. Past the drinks stands the **self-checkout**, after FamilyMart's: start on the tablet, whose choices are icon tiles, drag each product onto the scanner (or click it), go to payment, confirm you are 20 or over if there is alcohol, answer the points card and carrier bag screens (¥5), choose 現金 (you have no IC card or payment app, and your foreign card is refused), drag your notes and coins into their slots (¥1 and ¥5 coins are welcome here), confirm, and take your change from its two trays, notes and coins. The tablet also speaks English. What you buy appears in your bag, drawn as in the basket; it can't be eaten yet. 取消 on the tablet calls the sale off and brings it back to its welcome. As in the aisles, two buttons stand in the bottom corners of the till's picture, **LB** back to the shelves and **RB** out of the shop. **Esc** or **B** steps back; leaving with a full basket asks once, then puts it all back.

At a machine, the screen and the payment block stay visible together, laid out like a JR East machine at Tokyo Station: card/Suica slot, banknote slot and coin bowl under the screen, then the ticket outlet, the change outlet and a keypad. The wallet stands beside the machine. Drag a piece onto a slot, or click it (or **A** on a gamepad) to put it in its own slot. Notes do not fit the coin bowl, coins do not fit the banknote slot, and **¥1 and ¥5 coins are refused**: they drop straight into the change outlet. Change is given in as few pieces as possible. Cancelling, going back or leaving the machine returns the pieces inserted to the change outlet. The change comes out in two bins, the notes neatly stacked in one and the coins in a loose heap in the other: one click on a bin takes all it holds. Each machine keeps whatever is left in its outlets (ticket, change, refunds), even after you leave the hall or reload the page: come back to the same machine to pick it up. In the hall, a machine with something left in its outlets says so in the status line. Small synthesized sounds accompany each coin, note and refusal.

The **vending machines** in the street (two against the konbini, two more along the way home) open in first person too: a red one for sodas, ramune and hot soups, a blue one for coffee, a white one for tea and water, each drink over a blue strip for cold or a red one for hot. Drag coins or a ¥1,000 note from the purse onto the slots (or click them): ¥1 and ¥5 coins drop straight into the cup, larger notes are refused. The button under every drink you can afford lights up; press it and the drink drops into the hatch at the foot, your change into the cup; take them both. The 返却 lever gives your money back if you change your mind. Drinks go into your bag.

Every coin beyond eight **slows the walk** by 2.5%, down to 55%. Paying with small coins and getting a big one back lightens the purse; ¥1 and ¥5 coins can only be spent at a konbini's self-checkout.

## The Shinkansen

The Shinkansen transfer gates on the right of the concourse take a Shinkansen ticket bought at that station and lead straight up to its platform, at the end of its track: the same island platform as the local lines', where white sixteen-car Shinkansen pull in, doors at both ends of each car, and travellers queue at the marks. Board as on a local line; the line map lets you off at the stations with Shinkansen gates (Tokyo, Shinagawa, Kyoto and Shin-Osaka on the Tokaido Shinkansen, Tokyo, Ueno and Omiya on the Tohoku Shinkansen). The stairs of a Shinkansen platform lead straight to its transfer gates, facing out: the gate keeps the ticket of the trip and you are back in the concourse; a ticket that came in here without travelling comes back out unpunched (the entry is cancelled). Reloading on a Shinkansen platform puts you back in the concourse before its gates.

## Shinkansen tickets and excursions

Moving right along the row leads to the green **Shinkansen** machine, where the station has one: destination → date → 1 adult → train → seat preference → confirmation → payment from the wallet → ticket. It sells a ticket to every other station of its lines (from Tokyo: Shinagawa, Kyoto and Shin-Osaka, Ueno and Omiya), priced like a real one: the fare for the km, and the reserved-seat express charge (Tokyo → Kyoto ¥14,570). The trains on offer are two Nozomi or Hayabusa, the ride as long as the distance makes it. The reservation keeps these choices on the ticket. IC, QR, charging, bank cards and reservation pickup are visible but disabled.

Kamakura and Saga-Arashiyama are stations like the others, reached with a fare ticket: Kamakura's east exit (東口, Komachi-dori) walks to the temple trail, Saga-Arashiyama's to the bamboo path, and their **STATION** sign walks back into the station, at that exit. The way back is a fare ticket from its machines. Kawaguchiko was removed.

For Shinkansen tickets, the wrong train does not use up the ticket: ride back, or get off and take the other track. Platforms, timetables, reservations and train times remain fictional or simplified for the game; there is no real transaction.

## Finding your way and keeping memories

The hub concourses, seen from above, are laid out like the first-person ticket hall: at the back, the tiled wall with the station's name over the fare map (current station in red), the Kamakura and Nikko posters and the five machines under their lit signs; on the left, the みどりの窓口 ticket office and, beside it, the central gates to the local lines, set back under their 中央改札 sign, with the corridor to the platforms running on beyond them; on the right, the Shinkansen transfer gates, or, in a station without Shinkansen, a **NewDays** convenience store whose door people come and go through. Every station shares this layout and differs in its name, colour, exits, board and that right-hand side; without a Shinkansen, the fifth machine sells local tickets instead of Shinkansen ones. Two tiled pillars out in the concourse carry the yellow 出口 signs pointing to the west and east exits, whose names are also painted on the floor; people walk behind and in front of them. The floor, the yellow tactile strip and the commuters are the same as in the hall: each outfit of the first-person view comes and goes at random: in from an exit, off a train through the gates or out of the shop, sometimes stopping a few seconds at a free ticket machine, at a ticket office counter or to look around, then out through the gates, into the shop or out by an exit; two people stand reading. People are not walls: brushing past someone slows you both for a moment and nudges you aside, like squeezing through a crowd. Mostly Japanese signage, visual announcements and an animated crowd create a sense of confusion. The side exits lead straight out of the station, without a confirmation: Kamakura's east exit to the temple trail, every other one to a generic street outside the station, the same for every station: the station front with its entrance under a sign naming the station and the exit (東京駅 東口 / TOKYO STATION · EAST EXIT), a konbini beside it (the same shop everywhere under the sign of a 7-Eleven, FamilyMart or Lawson, each station keeping its own chain), a plaza with vending machines, planters, an area map dressed like the guide maps by Tokyo's stations (a dark frame, two white panels ringed in blue pointing to the station and the bus stop, the legend down the right; current spot in red; read up close, from in front of it or beside it, it shows the street itself at a lower resolution, you on a red 現在地 spot, and on its own face the same picture again, smaller and coarser each time) and a bus stop, the road and its crossing, and a row of shops across it. Walking back through the doors returns to the concourse at the exit you took. Only some stations will get their own map for an exit; the shops and streets are not open yet. The subway shown on the signs is decorative and not playable. There is no time limit; the only penalty is the weight of your coins, and the only audio comes from the ticket machines.

On the Kamakura temple trail and in Arashiyama, walk up to the camera and interact to collect a memory. Memories persist in localStorage on this device. The images are placeholders; importing personal photos is not implemented.

## Phone export

`npm run build:mobile` produces `dist/yorimichi-mobile.html`, a standalone file containing the game, its scripts, its SVG map and its styles. It does not need to load any external file. Open it in landscape on a phone to read the machine comfortably.

To choose another export path after building: `node scripts/export-mobile.mjs /path/yorimichi-mobile.html`.

## In front of the machines

By default this view is **pixel art**, like the gate close-up: the machine bank seen from the concourse, about four machines at a time from the foot of the board down to the floor, with the station's name and fare board above (the Tokyo fare map stays the crisp SVG laid over the board) and the concourse's commuters standing at the machines or passing in front. **← / →** (or A/D, the D-pad, the left stick; hold to keep going) walk along the bank from machine to machine, the picture following with its perspective; the machine in front is framed in yellow. **↑** raises the view to the board (at Tokyo the wall map, framed in yellow) and **↓** brings it back to the machines. A click, **E** or **A** uses what is framed; a click on another machine walks there, and a second click uses it. Busy machines, the enlarged map and Esc / **B** work as below. Adding `?hall=3d` to the address brings back the first-person 3D scene described next, unchanged; `?hall=voxel` shows that same scene, with the same controls, built of lit and shadowed voxels: the wall tiles, machines, signs (their lettering raised voxel by voxel), gates, ticket office, posters and the commuters, carved from their sprites so they turn and step in 3D.

A first-person scene, rendered in 3D with three.js at low resolution and scaled up without smoothing to keep the pixels of the rest of the game: a piece of station around a tiled wall, a large fare board under the station's name (as on the top-down map) and five machines set into a pale façade (a large Japanese/English light sign above each, a tilted screen, a payment block with card/Suica slots, banknotes, ticket and change outlets, coin slot, keypad, QR reader and tray, metal ledge), with every texture drawn in code using the game's palette. The fare map and machine signs stay crisp; the payment blocks are double-density pixel art, shown outside the low-resolution render to stay legible. Around them, the station carries on so the scenery never seems to end: tiled pillars, a dark ceiling with fluorescent lights, a yellow tactile strip, hanging signs (きっぷうりば, 中央改札, みどりの窓口, 新幹線のりかえ), travel posters for Kamakura, Nikko (the Yōmei-mon gate of Tōshō-gū) and Arashiyama, on the left the みどりの窓口 counter beside the entry gates, the yellow 出口 exit signs wrapped around the pillars on either side, on the right the Shinkansen transfer gates set back past the pillar or, at Kamakura, the NewDays front, and passers-by who are all different (women and men, schoolchildren, elderly people, kimono, masks). They are the concourse's own commuters, seen where they are on the top-down map at that moment: someone at the next machine, people going through the gates or waiting at the ticket office, the two who stand reading. You cannot use a machine while someone is at it: wait for them to finish or take another. Nobody comes to the machine you stand at, and anyone heading there turns to another; a light haze fades out the background. You can step closer to the machines, and back only a few steps. The machine screens use a pixel skin (DotGothic16 font, hard edges, bevels): local tickets, IC charge, local tickets, IC charge, Shinkansen (local tickets again at Kamakura). The reference photos provided guide the layout; they are not included in the game. The wall map shows the same 30 stations with fares as a coloured network, with station names in Japanese only, laid out like the JR East boards above the machines: Yamanote loop in the centre, red arrow on Tokyo, legend and fare list on the left. A Japanese font is embedded so the map stays legible offline; no new fares were added.
The Yamanote forms a closed loop with its 30 stops. The map also shows the names of the intermediate stops on the lines around Tokyo, taken from the official JR East map. You read the board up close. Fares remain limited to the 30 stations with fares in the JR East reference, plus Kamakura.

- **Phone**: drag on the scenery to look around; tap a machine to walk to it and then use it, tap the map to read it. There are no buttons around the view: it fills the screen.
- **Reading the map**: clicking the map (or E / A while looking at it) opens the SVG full size: every station shows its name and its fare from the station you are in. Drag the map and zoom with the wheel (towards the cursor) or by pinching with two fingers, down to the whole map fitting the screen; on a gamepad, the sticks pan and **LT / RT** zoom out / in; **Close**, Esc or **B** goes back to where you came from: the machine screen if you opened the map from it, otherwise in front of the machines.
- **Mouse and keyboard**: click and drag to look, left/right arrows or A/D to move sideways, up/down or W/S to step closer/back, R/F or Page Up/Down to look up/down (keys read by position: ZQSD on an AZERTY keyboard). Wheel: distance; Shift + wheel or horizontal scroll: sideways movement. E/Space uses the machine in front of you, or opens the map if you are looking at it; Esc goes back to the concourse.
- **Stepping back out**: from the farthest spot, keep stepping back (S/down, the wheel or the stick) for a moment and you turn back to the concourse, as with Esc; the same goes for walking on sideways past either end of the machine bank (A/D, left/right, the wheel or the stick). The status line warns you first.
- **Standard gamepad**: left stick horizontal to move sideways, vertical for distance; right stick to look around. A uses the machine in front of you, or opens the map if you are looking at it; B goes back. On the purchase screen, the D-pad/left stick moves focus between the machine's buttons, its outlets and the wallet, A presses the button, Y opens the wall map full size (the button shows a round **Y** badge) and B steps back like the machine's **Back** button, returning any money inserted; on the home screen, or once the ticket is issued, B leaves the machine. These controls were checked with simulated input in Chromium, not with a physical gamepad.

The two pink **チャージ専用** machines open their own screen, like the others: it asks for an IC card (Suica, PASMO) in the card slot, its **Charge** and **Check history** buttons stay off, and the slots refuse money and cards alike. The player has no IC card and these machines do not sell any, so nothing can be done there yet.

The purchase stays paused while you look at the map and resumes at the same machine. Choosing another machine restarts its own screen; a ticket already issued is kept. Cancelling or taking the ticket brings you back to the machines; **Esc** or **B** (or stepping back from the machines) returns to top-down exploration. In Kyoto, this view shows simple local signage rather than the Tokyo fare map.
